NIS2 and IoT: What German Manufacturers Must Know Before the Deadline

Gulam 'Enkay' Samdani · 1 June 2025 · 12 min read
NIS2 IoT Security OT Security German Compliance Mittelstand

The NIS2 Directive has been German law since October 2024. The NIS2UmsuCG (NIS2-Umsetzungs- und Cybersicherheitsstärkungsgesetz) transposes the EU directive into national legislation, with the BSI (Bundesamt für Sicherheit in der Informationstechnik) as the enforcement authority. Fines reach €10 million or 2% of global annual turnover — whichever is higher — for Essential Entities. That is not a regulatory nuisance. That is an existential risk for mid-sized manufacturers.

An estimated 29,000+ German companies are now in scope. Most of them are not ready. And the biggest gap — the one that shows up in every assessment we run — is IoT and OT device security.

Key Point

NIS2 is technology-neutral. It applies to your connected sensors, PLCs, HMIs, edge gateways, and SCADA systems just as much as it applies to your ERP servers. If a device touches a network and is part of your operational infrastructure, it is in scope.

Are You In Scope? The Two-Threshold Test

NIS2UmsuCG distinguishes between two entity categories. Your category determines your obligations and the maximum fine applicable to you. The test is size-first: sector classification only matters once you cross the size threshold.

Category Staff Annual Turnover Max Fine
Essential Entity (§28) ≥ 250 ≥ €50M €10M or 2% global turnover
Important Entity (§29) ≥ 50 ≥ €10M €7M or 1.4% global turnover
Below threshold < 50 < €10M Exempt (exceptions apply to critical infrastructure)

Sectors explicitly named in Annex I (Essential) include energy, transport, banking, health, digital infrastructure, and manufacturing of critical products — including vehicles, medical devices, and certain electrical equipment. Annex II (Important) adds postal services, waste management, chemicals, food, and general manufacturing.

If you supply into automotive, aerospace, pharmaceutical, or medical device supply chains and have more than 50 employees, there is a high probability you are an Important Entity. If you operate industrial processes with networked control systems and have more than 250 employees, you are almost certainly an Essential Entity.

The practical implication: many Tier-2 and Tier-3 suppliers who have never engaged with IT security regulation at this level are now directly obligated — not indirectly via their customers' TISAX requirements, but directly, by law, to the BSI.

Why IoT Is the Highest-Risk Compliance Gap

Traditional IT security (firewalls, EDR, patch management, access control) is reasonably well understood in manufacturing. The CISO function, where it exists, handles these. IoT and OT security is different — and it's different in ways that make it disproportionately dangerous under NIS2.

OT/IoT devices were designed for function, not security. A temperature sensor installed in 2017 was spec'd for accuracy, power consumption, and MTBF. Nobody asked whether it ran an authenticated MQTT broker, whether its firmware could be updated over the air, or whether its default credentials had ever been changed. In most factories, the honest answer to all three questions is: no, no, and no.

NIS2 is technology-neutral. It does not exempt legacy devices. The attack surface your IoT fleet represents includes:

  • Unencrypted telemetry — MQTT on port 1883 without TLS, plain HTTP REST callbacks, Modbus TCP in clear text across production VLANs
  • Default credentials — factory-default admin/admin on web interfaces that have never been rotated because "it's on the internal network"
  • No firmware update path — devices that cannot receive security patches, either because the vendor no longer supports them or because the update process requires physical access and has never been scheduled
  • Flat network topology — IT and OT on the same Layer 3 broadcast domain, meaning a compromised office workstation has direct network access to PLCs and HMIs
  • No monitoring — no SIEM coverage of OT traffic, no anomaly detection on industrial protocols, no visibility into what is communicating with what

In a recent assessment of a mid-sized Rhineland parts manufacturer, we found 47 networked devices — sensors, cameras, gateways, a CNC controller, and several legacy HMIs — that had never appeared in any asset inventory. The IT team knew about 12 of them. NIS2 requires you to know about all of them.

What NIS2 Article 21 Actually Requires

Article 21 of the NIS2 Directive (implemented in §30 NIS2UmsuCG) sets out the minimum cybersecurity risk management measures. In plain language, these are:

1. Asset inventory

You must know what you have. This sounds obvious. It is routinely absent for OT/IoT assets. A compliant asset inventory for a manufacturing facility needs to cover every networked device — including those on the shop floor, in logistics, and in building management systems.

A practical starting point is passive network discovery. Tools like nmap can help map your current state:

# Discover devices on production VLAN without sending probes that could disrupt OT devices
nmap -sn -T2 --script=broadcast-arp-scan 192.168.10.0/24

# Identify open ports and service versions on discovered hosts
nmap -sV -p 80,443,1883,8883,502,47808 --script=banner 192.168.10.0/24 -oN ot-inventory.txt

Use -T2 (polite timing) on OT networks. Aggressive scans can crash older PLCs and HMI controllers. The output feeds your asset register, which must be maintained and kept current.

2. Encryption in transit

All IoT telemetry must be encrypted in transit. For MQTT-based devices, this means migrating from port 1883 (plain text) to port 8883 (MQTT over TLS 1.3). For HTTP-based REST integrations, TLS 1.2 minimum, TLS 1.3 preferred. Modbus TCP and legacy industrial protocols require network-level segmentation and encryption at the gateway layer if the protocol itself cannot be secured.

3. Supply chain security

NIS2 Article 21(2)(d) explicitly requires security in supply chain relationships, including with suppliers and service providers. For IoT, this means: reviewing the security posture of device vendors, verifying firmware signing, and assessing whether vendors provide documented vulnerability disclosure and patch release processes.

4. Incident detection and notification

§35 NIS2UmsuCG requires a 24-hour early warning to the BSI after becoming aware of a significant incident, and a 72-hour full notification. You cannot meet these timelines without monitoring. A significant incident involving an IoT device — a compromised sensor that becomes a pivot point into your OT network, for example — triggers these obligations. Without logging and anomaly detection on your IoT fleet, you will not know an incident has occurred until it is too late to meet the reporting window.

5. Business continuity

You must be able to demonstrate continuity plans that account for cybersecurity incidents. For IoT-dependent processes — cold chain monitoring, production line sensors, quality control systems — this means documented fallback procedures for sensor failure, communication loss, and data integrity loss scenarios.

A Practical 90-Day Roadmap

For manufacturers starting from a typical baseline — reasonable IT security, limited OT visibility, ad-hoc IoT deployments — here is a realistic 12-week compliance approach:

  • Weeks 1–2: Asset discovery — passive network scan across all VLANs including OT segments. Build your device register. Identify firmware versions, protocols, and communication paths.
  • Weeks 3–4: Network segmentation assessment — map the boundary between IT and OT networks. Identify and document any flat segments where office and production devices share the same broadcast domain.
  • Weeks 5–6: Encryption audit — inventory all MQTT, HTTP, and industrial protocol connections. Flag everything running without TLS. Plan migration for MQTT devices to port 8883 with TLS 1.3.
  • Weeks 6–7: Credential review — audit default and shared credentials across all IoT and OT devices. Rotate factory defaults. Implement password management for device credentials where the device supports it.
  • Weeks 8–10: Monitoring and detection — implement network-level logging for OT/IoT traffic. Configure anomaly alerts for unusual communication patterns. Establish SIEM coverage for device events where feasible.
  • Weeks 10–12: Policy and documentation — produce written incident response procedures including the 24h/72h BSI notification workflow. Document business continuity plans for critical IoT-dependent processes.

Where Companies Are Failing

In the assessments we have completed since NIS2UmsuCG came into force, three failure patterns repeat consistently:

1. Assuming IT security covers OT. The IT team has CrowdStrike on every endpoint, a SOC contract, and a mature patch process. None of that touches the ESP32 gateway on the production line, the IP camera network that never got a VLAN, or the old Siemens HMI that runs Windows XP and cannot be patched without a six-month vendor engagement.

2. Treating NIS2 as a documentation exercise. Gap analysis reports, policy documents, and risk registers are necessary but not sufficient. The BSI can and will test technical controls. A policy that says "all MQTT connections shall use TLS" while port 1883 is open and trafficked is not compliance — it is liability.

3. Missing the supply chain obligation. Buying a device from a vendor does not transfer your NIS2 obligation to them. If a device in your facility cannot receive security patches, that is your risk to manage. Vendor contracts should now include documented patch SLAs, CVE disclosure timelines, and end-of-security-support dates.

BSI Registration Requirement

Many manufacturers are unaware of §33 NIS2UmsuCG: Essential and Important Entities must register with the BSI within 3 months of becoming obligated. The BSI registration portal opened in early 2025. Registration requires basic entity information, sector classification, and contact details for security incident notification.

Failure to register is itself a regulatory violation — separate from any substantive security failure. If you have not registered, this should be your first action.

Action Required

BSI registration is mandatory and has a 3-month deadline from the date your organisation becomes obligated. Registration is separate from technical compliance. Both are required. Check your registration status at meldung.bsi.de.

Summary: Five Things To Do This Week

  1. Determine your entity classification. Are you Essential (≥250 staff, ≥€50M turnover) or Important (≥50 staff, ≥€10M turnover)? If uncertain, assume Important and proceed accordingly.
  2. Check your BSI registration status. If you are obligated and not registered, register now. The portal is at meldung.bsi.de.
  3. Run a basic asset discovery on your OT/IoT network. You cannot manage what you cannot see. Use passive scanning on production VLANs.
  4. Identify any MQTT connections on port 1883 or unencrypted HTTP IoT endpoints. These are your highest-priority remediation items under Article 21's encryption requirement.
  5. Document your incident notification workflow. Who, in your organisation, is responsible for deciding that a cybersecurity event is "significant"? How does that trigger the 24h BSI early warning? Write it down before you need it.

NIS2 compliance is not a one-time project. It is an ongoing operational posture. The companies that treat it that way — building monitoring, maintaining inventories, running regular audits — will find compliance manageable. The companies that treat it as a checkbox exercise will face audit findings, enforcement action, and, eventually, an incident that demonstrates exactly what they failed to prevent.
